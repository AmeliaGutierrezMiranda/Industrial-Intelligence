# Recruiting Civil and Structural Engineers in California
## What makes the search difficult—and what I need to uncover

*Recruiter field notes | Amelia Gutierrez Miranda | Updated October 19, 2026*

I recruit nationally, but I build each search locally. California engineering searches have reinforced something important: a technically qualified person is not automatically a movable candidate.

When my coworkers and I struggle to attract experienced civil, structural and building electrical professionals, I need to understand what they would be leaving behind—and what the new opportunity actually improves.

These notes combine recruiting observations, questions to test, and public sources. They do not establish why any particular engineer stays with an employer. Search examples are anonymized; no confidential candidate information belongs in this public guide.

## 1. The challenge starts with defining the work

| Search example | What I need to clarify before sourcing |
|---|---|
| Civil engineer with approximately 8 years of experience | Site development versus transportation, water or another specialty; grading, drainage, utilities, stormwater, permitting and project ownership |
| Senior civil engineer with 12+ years | Technical depth versus project management, client leadership, mentoring, business development and an ownership path |
| Building electrical designer or engineer | Power, lighting and distribution; designer versus licensed engineer; which healthcare, school or commercial experience is essential |
| Structural lead moving into a director scope | Existing-building repair, seismic retrofit, foundations, retaining walls, hillside work, field judgment, review authority and people leadership |

Mixed-use, healthcare, schools and commercial buildings describe sectors. They do not fully describe what the engineer must deliver. I need the project types, systems, materials, approval requirements and actual responsibilities.

A change from senior lead to director requires a new intake. A larger title needs a matching discussion about decision-making authority, staffing, compensation and accountability.

## 2. Why long tenure may make sense

Long tenure does not prove an engineer is under contract or unwilling to move. Possible reasons to test include:

- Trust in the engineers who review their work.
- Established clients, project ownership and local agency relationships.
- A credible principal, ownership or leadership opportunity.
- Predictable workload, commute and flexibility.
- Reliable production support and quality-control practices.
- Bonus timing, retirement vesting or ownership arrangements.
- Valuable mentorship and qualifying experience toward licensure.

The recruiting question is: **What meaningful improvement would justify rebuilding that trust somewhere else?**

## 3. PE/SE support is not ownership of the license

An employer may provide mentorship, qualifying assignments and references, and may reimburse exam preparation, fees or renewals. Confirm each firm's actual policy.

California civil licensure includes qualifying experience and examinations, including state seismic and surveying exams. California SE authority requires an active California civil license and additional qualifying structural experience and examination requirements. The Board's current rules control; experience and references require individual review.

A license belongs to the individual. Employer support does not by itself establish a multi-year obligation to stay. Do not assume a repayment agreement exists, or draw conclusions about enforceability without appropriate review.

## 4. How bonuses can affect mobility

Project results can influence an annual bonus without producing a separate payment at project completion.

| Arrangement to clarify with the hiring employer | Possible basis | Payment question |
|---|---|---|
| Annual discretionary bonus | Individual contribution and business results | When is it decided and paid? Is there a target or guarantee? |
| Branch or office pool | Office financial performance | How is the pool funded and allocated? |
| Individual incentive | Delivery, leadership, client development or other defined goals | What is the formula, measurement period and first-year eligibility? |
| Project incentive | Margin, milestones, budget or collections | Is payment tied to closeout, milestones or collections? |
| Profit sharing | Firm profitability and plan rules | Is this cash or a retirement contribution? |
| Ownership distributions | Ownership interest and firm results | What are eligibility, buy-in, vesting and distribution terms? |

These are structures to investigate, not a claim that every engineering firm uses them.

Public examples show why terminology matters: Coffman advertises an annual discretionary bonus; CEC lists performance bonuses, profit sharing and ownership; Bono Consulting distinguishes a year-end performance bonus from retirement-plan profit sharing. Public benefits pages do not reveal every calculation or eligibility term.

For project economics, ask the client how budgeted hours, rework, scope changes, collections and quality affect incentives. Do not assume engineers receive a percentage of project profit.

## 5. What candidates may be reluctant to discuss

Reluctance can reflect privacy, confidentiality or uncertainty. It is not evidence of dishonesty. My goal is enough information to assess fit—not access to private employer records.

| Possible unspoken concern | Why it may be sensitive | Respectful question | Recruiting response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timing of a possible move | Financial or project commitments | “What start-date window would work for you?” | Discuss timing before promising an immediate start |
| A hoped-for promotion or ownership opportunity | It may be informal or uncertain | “What would you want your next role to offer in advancement or ownership?” | Get the new employer's path and criteria clearly defined |
| Fear of losing technical support | Criticizing a current team can feel risky | “What review process and technical support would you need to do your best work?” | Explain actual reviewers, staffing and escalation |
| Concern about stamping or technical responsibility | Professional reputation and accountability | “What would you need clarified about responsible charge, review authority and project acceptance?” | Arrange a technical leadership conversation |
| Workload or burnout | Fear of being judged as unable to handle pressure | “What workload and delivery expectations would be sustainable in your next role?” | Verify hours, staffing and deadlines with the client |
| Commute or schedule limitations | Personal reasons may be private | “Which office days, travel territory and schedule could you commit to?” | Assess availability without asking for personal explanations |
| Client loyalty and unfinished work | Professional relationships and confidentiality | “What transition period would you want to provide?” | Build a realistic handoff window |
| Interest in technical work rather than management | A director title may be unattractive | “What mix of design, review, fieldwork and people leadership do you want?” | Match the actual work rather than sell title alone |
| A possible counteroffer or change of mind | They may still be exploring | “What would have to be true for you to accept and follow through?” | Resolve decision criteria early |
| Fear that a search will become public | A small professional network | “How should we handle confidentiality, and when may I share your information?” | Obtain permission before submission or reference outreach |

A candidate can decline to share details. Record “not discussed” or “unknown,” not a guessed explanation.

## 6. California compensation questions: an important boundary

California generally prohibits employers and their agents from seeking applicant salary history, including compensation and benefits. Asking about expectations for the prospective role is permitted. See the California Department of Industrial Relations guidance linked below.

This corrects an overly broad recruiting approach: do not ask applicants for their current bonus structure, prior payouts, current benefits or compensation documents.

Use forward-looking questions instead:

- “The base range is X–Y, with this documented incentive plan. Does that align with your expectations?”
- “What base and total compensation expectations do you have for this opportunity?”
- “Would you require a sign-on payment or first-year guarantee to accept this role?”
- “What start date would work for you?”
- “Which parts of this proposed package need clarification?”

Do not use a question about a lost bonus as a workaround to obtain compensation history. If a candidate volunteers information, do not probe for additional history; follow employer policy and applicable guidance.

## 7. What I must uncover from the hiring company

Before presenting the opportunity, I need concrete answers:

- **Scope:** What projects will the hire own in the first six months?
- **Authority:** Can the director hire, allocate staff, set review standards or decline unsuitable work?
- **Support:** Who performs design, calculations, drafting and independent review?
- **Accountability:** What are stamping, responsible-charge and professional liability coverage expectations?
- **Workload:** What are actual delivery expectations and staffing constraints?
- **Location:** What is the exact office address, required office schedule and field territory?
- **Compensation:** What are the base range, bonus terms, payment timing and first-year eligibility?
- **Advancement:** What measurable steps lead to leadership or ownership?
- **Licensure:** Which credentials are required on day one, and what support is available?
- **Reason to move:** What can this firm offer that the candidate cannot obtain internally?

“Great culture,” “growth” and “bonus eligible” need supporting facts.

## 8. My discovery sequence

1. Confirm the candidate's actual technical work and applicable credentials.
2. Ask what they want more of—and less of—in their next role.
3. Present the verified scope, location and compensation range.
4. Discuss expectations, confidentiality and a workable start window.
5. Arrange technical discussion about review, staffing and authority.
6. Confirm remaining conditions before submission, interview progression and offer acceptance.

**Opening question:** “What would need to be meaningfully better for you to consider leaving a firm where you already have credibility?”

## 9. Search tracking template

Keep individual records in an approved private recruiting system, never in this public repository.

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Technical match | Relevant project types, systems, materials and responsibilities |
| Credentials | Verified license status and requirements still to resolve |
| Move motivation | Candidate's stated priorities |
| Compensation expectations | Expectations for the prospective role |
| Timing | Preferred start window |
| Work arrangement | Agreed office, field and travel availability |
| Technical confidence | Questions about support, review and authority |
| Outstanding conditions | Candidate-stated requirements; distinguish unknowns |
| Permission | Submission and reference-contact permissions |
| Next step | Owner and agreed date |

A strong candidate who will not move is different from a poorly matched candidate. Track technical fit and readiness separately.
