# Industrial Intelligence

I recruit in manufacturing, engineering, skilled trades and industrial environments. The more I work in these spaces, the more obvious it becomes that you cannot recruit the right person if you do not understand what the operation actually does.

A job title by itself is rarely enough.

A Manufacturing Engineer in aerospace may not look exactly like one in food production. A Maintenance Technician in a highly automated facility may need a completely different skill set than someone working in a smaller mechanical environment. A CNC candidate who has worked on one type of machine may still be highly transferable to another operation if you understand the process behind the equipment.

That is what I mean by **Industrial Intelligence**.

It is the ability to look beyond the job description and understand the relationship between:

**Facility → Process → Equipment → Skills → Job Titles → Transferable Industries → Local Talent Market**

## Why I Built This

I am a recruiter, not an engineer, machinist or maintenance technician. My job is not to pretend I know more than the people doing the work.

My job is to ask enough of the right questions to understand what makes someone successful in that environment.

When I enter a new manufacturing market, I want to know:

- What does the company actually make?
- How does the product move through the facility?
- What machines, systems and processes are involved?
- Which skills are truly required and which ones can be trained?
- What other industries use similar equipment or processes?
- Which employers nearby are developing similar talent?
- How far will people realistically commute for this type of work?
- Where do these workers actually spend time online and offline?

Once I understand those pieces, sourcing becomes much more strategic.

## The Industrial Intelligence Framework

### 1. Understand the Operation

Start with the business before the job title.

What does the facility produce? Is the environment machining, fabrication, assembly, food production, packaging, mining, plastics, aerospace, automotive, building products or something else?

The product often tells you where to begin looking for transferable talent.

### 2. Follow the Process

Understand how work moves through the facility.

Raw material → production process → quality → maintenance → finished product.

This helps identify the people who touch the operation at different stages and the skills that connect those jobs.

### 3. Identify the Equipment

Equipment can tell you more than a title.

Examples include:

- CNC mills and lathes
- PLC-controlled equipment
- Robotics and automation
- Conveyors
- Hydraulic and pneumatic systems
- CMM and inspection equipment
- Welding and fabrication equipment
- Packaging lines
- Pumps, motors and drives
- Heavy industrial and mining equipment

### 4. Translate Equipment Into Skills

Instead of searching only for job titles, search for what people actually know how to do.

A maintenance candidate may have experience with motors, conveyors, VFDs, PLC troubleshooting, hydraulics and pneumatics even if their title was not "Industrial Maintenance Technician."

A manufacturing candidate may understand process improvement, CNC operations, tooling, quality systems and production flow even if their previous employer used a different title.

### 5. Map Transferable Industries

Good candidates do not always come from direct competitors.

Similar processes can exist across industries that look completely different on the surface.

For example, automation, preventive maintenance, quality systems and production engineering may transfer across:

- Automotive
- Aerospace
- Metal fabrication
- Food and beverage
- Plastics
- Packaging
- Building products
- Industrial equipment
- Mining
- Semiconductor manufacturing

The question becomes less **"Have they worked for this exact type of company?"** and more **"Have they worked with a comparable process, level of complexity and equipment?"**

### 6. Understand the Local Labor Market

The same recruiting strategy will not work everywhere.

A candidate pool in Phoenix behaves differently than one in a smaller Wisconsin manufacturing community. LinkedIn participation, commute tolerance, wages, shift preferences, local employers, trade schools and word-of-mouth networks all matter.

Sometimes the best sourcing strategy is LinkedIn. Sometimes it is Facebook, Indeed, a technical school, an employee referral, a community group or direct outreach into adjacent employers.

The market tells you where to look.

## The Goal

Industrial recruiting should not be keyword matching.

The better I understand the environment where someone will actually work, the better I can recognize transferable experience, explain the opportunity honestly and connect the right people to the right operation.

This repository documents that learning process.
