# Market Mapping Framework

When I start recruiting in a market I do not know well, I do not want to just pull a radius around a ZIP code and start searching titles.

I want to understand why the talent is there in the first place.

## Step 1: Start With the Facility

Before sourcing, answer the basics:

- What does the company make or service?
- Is the operation high-volume, custom, automated, manual or mixed?
- What happens on the production floor?
- Which departments keep the operation moving?
- What shifts are involved?

This gives context to every job title that comes after it.

## Step 2: Identify the Core Processes

Look for the work happening inside the operation, such as:

- Machining
- Fabrication
- Welding
- Assembly
- Packaging
- Material handling
- Maintenance
- Quality inspection
- Process engineering
- Automation
- Heavy equipment operation

The process is often more useful than the company name when looking for transferable candidates.

## Step 3: Build the Equipment Vocabulary

List the equipment and systems candidates may mention on a resume or in conversation.

Examples:

- CNC mills and lathes
- Fanuc, Haas, Mazak or Okuma controls
- PLCs
- VFDs
- Servo systems
- Robotics
- Conveyors
- Pumps and motors
- Hydraulics and pneumatics
- CMMs
- Calipers and micrometers
- Presses
- Welders
- Packaging equipment

This becomes sourcing vocabulary.

## Step 4: Separate Must-Haves From Transferable Skills

One of the easiest ways to shrink a candidate pool unnecessarily is treating every preference as a requirement.

I separate:

**Must have** — something the person truly needs on day one.

**Transferable** — experience that gives them the foundation to learn the environment.

**Trainable** — something the employer can reasonably teach.

That distinction can completely change the size of the market.

## Step 5: Map Adjacent Employers

Direct competitors are only one part of the search.

I look for employers using similar:

- Equipment
- Production processes
- Materials
- Maintenance systems
- Quality standards
- Automation
- Engineering disciplines

A candidate may come from a different product category but still have the exact operational experience needed.

## Step 6: Understand Geography Like a Worker

A recruiting radius on a screen is not the same thing as a realistic commute.

Questions I consider:

- Where do workers actually live?
- Which highways or roads affect the commute?
- Is 30 miles normal here or unrealistic?
- Are there nearby towns people commute between every day?
- Does shift timing make the commute easier or harder?
- Is relocation realistic for this level of role?

## Step 7: Learn Where the Workforce Is Visible

Not every skilled workforce lives on LinkedIn.

Depending on the market, useful sources can include:

- LinkedIn
- Indeed
- Facebook groups
- Trade schools and community colleges
- Professional associations
- Local workforce programs
- Employee referrals
- Community organizations
- Direct employer mapping

Low digital visibility does not mean low skill.

## Step 8: Talk to People and Adjust

The map gets better with every conversation.

Candidates tell you things a database cannot:

- Which companies pay well
- Which shifts people avoid
- Which plants train
- Which employers have good reputations
- Which equipment is common locally
- Why people leave
- How far workers are willing to commute

That information feeds the search back into itself.

## My Working Formula

**Understand the operation → identify the process → learn the equipment → translate it into skills → map transferable employers → understand the geography → find where the workforce is visible → keep learning from the people in the market.**

That is how I approach industrial market mapping.
